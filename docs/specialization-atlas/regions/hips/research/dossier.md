# Specialization atlas: hip anatomy and its evidence (19 Oct 2026)

What the hips' anatomy content would say, what it rests on, and how far that review can be trusted. This is the anatomy lane's research dossier for wave E; the content files it feeds are `shared/specialization/regions/hips/{nodes,nodeActions,claims}.ts` and `shared/specialization/sources/hips.ts`. Ids, names, short names, kinds, roles, layers, parents and region actions are those fixed in 10-multi-region.md §1.2; the method, limits and wording follow 03-anatomy-evidence.md. The machine-readable form, with every query and the excerpt relied on for each citation, is `dossier.json` beside this file; the raw search log is `searchlog.md`. Nothing here reaches the app or the database until the hips' anatomy lane has turned it into content and that has passed its own adversarial review. An independent automated second review on 19 Oct 2026 corrected this dossier in place; what it changed and why is in §13 and in `secondReview` in `dossier.json`.

**In short.** 13 nodes, 33 claims (13 function, 13 appearance, 5 selectivity, 2 variation), all 33 reviewed and none draft; 24 node actions (12 prime, 12 assist); no new joint action (all six hip actions are already in `shared/specialization/actions/joints.ts`); 62 sources, every one cited by a claim; 115 citations, each with its query and excerpt; 2 deviations from §1.2, neither of which changes an id. The second review reworded 16 claims, sent none to draft, and added 3 sources (§13). Two product questions are open (§6): the tensor fasciae latae has no main action on the evidence, so the content test 'gives every muscle a main action' will fail for it, and no hip muscle is a main mover of inward rotation.

## 1. Method

**What was reviewed.** Every node of the hips in §1.2: the gluteal muscles (a parent) and the gluteus maximus, medius and minimus; the tensor fasciae latae; the piriformis (adjacent); the iliopsoas; the adductor group (a parent) and the adductor magnus, longus and brevis; the pectineus and gracilis (adjacent). For each, a function and an appearance claim, plus selectivity claims where a citable EMG finding was found (five) and variation claims where a real difference between people was found (two). The external members, the rectus femoris and the three hamstrings, belong to the thigh lane and are not researched here.

**How.** Web search only, as for the forearm. Page fetches are refused by the network proxy, so each claim was checked against what the search results showed: titles, URLs and the tool's generated summaries with quoted excerpts. 58 searches were run (Q1 to Q58 in `searchlog.md`), batched by muscle and by question. For each claim:

1. Search for reputable references in the evidence standard's order: NCBI Bookshelf (StatPearls), peer-reviewed papers, university and teaching pages (TeachMeAnatomy, the Stanford Bassett collection, a University of British Columbia dissection image, the 1918 Gray's Anatomy), Kenhub, Elsevier Complete Anatomy, Radiopaedia, IMAIOS. Wikipedia, WikiMSK and Physiopedia appeared in results and were read; none is cited.
2. Add each relied-on reference with a `<publisher>.<topic>` key, or `pmid.<number>` where the PubMed id was shown, and a note saying exactly what the search showed and what it could not confirm.
3. Cite it on the claim with a locator (the section the excerpt came from) and a qualification where the source is limited (search excerpt or summary, location only, abstract only, acute EMG, a video transcript, image labels).
4. Record, for every citation, the query and the excerpt relied on, verbatim from the search output (`citations` in the JSON; fragments of one result are joined with ' ... '). A script checked that every excerpt fragment appears word for word in `searchlog.md`, and that every citation's query is a logged query.
5. Mark a claim `reviewed` only when the recorded excerpts support every sentence, with `reviewedAt: "2026-10-19"` and the note "Checked against the cited sources through web search on 19 Oct 2026; automated review, not a clinician or anatomist." Appearance claims carry the forearm's appearance note adapted to the date and are always `anatomical_inference`.
6. A node is `reviewed` only when all its function claims are. Parents (the gluteals and the adductors) have a function claim only because the references speak of each group as such (TeachMeAnatomy and Kenhub on the gluteal muscles; Kenhub and StatPearls on the hip adductors); they have no node actions.

Wording was rewritten until each sentence said no more than its excerpts. Where references disagree (rotation by the adductors and the iliopsoas, the tensor fasciae latae at the knee, the piriformis's turning point), the claim says that they disagree and the action is left out of the node actions. Where an action changes with hip flexion and the evidence says so (the piriformis, the gluteus minimus, the adductor longus), the claim says so plainly rather than picking one. A build script also checked the copy rules of `regionContent.test.ts` and `forearmAnatomy.test.ts` over every node field, statement, locator and qualification: no per-cent sign, no growth promise, no 'optimal' or 'guarantee', no vendor name, no 'table', no screen left or right; deep target muscles say 'little or no' contour; surface target muscles' shape notes are qualified.

## 2. Limits

- **Excerpts and summaries, not full texts.** StatPearls showed introductions and parts of Structure and Function; several articles were behind a registration wall past that. Many results came as generated summaries with a few quoted lines, and the Kenhub videos as transcript summaries. Each citation's qualification says which.
- **Search summaries can misquote.** Function claims rest on two to five references each. Lines a summary did not tie to a named page were not used (for example a line calling the adductor longus 'the most anterior and superficial of the adductor muscles', Q52, and a Radiopaedia function sentence, Q54). Some figures came only from secondary summaries of an abstract (the 2013 gluteal-versus-TFL EMG study), and the claim's qualification says so.
- **Image labels.** The adductor magnus's back-view outcrop rests on the labels of two posterior dissection images described by a search summary; the images were not seen.
- **Automated review.** "Reviewed" here means checked by this automated process against search results. It is not a review by a clinician or an anatomist, and the database lane should not publish it as clinically verified on the strength of it.
- **Second review.** A separate automated agent re-checked every claim against its excerpts and re-searched 19 of the 33 claims with its own queries (SR1–SR32 in `secondReview.queries`). It found one first-pass source whose own results contradicted the claim it was cited for (the piriformis ultrasound abstract), one quoted phrase the abstract does not appear to contain ('the largest capacity'), and several descriptive titles it could now identify. It has the same limits as the first pass.
- **Nothing here is hypertrophy evidence.** The five selectivity claims cite acute EMG studies. They say what was measured and that it is not growth, and they must never become set-credit weights. No longitudinal growth study is cited.

Said plainly, as §8 requires on every region's "How this is counted":

> "Reviewed" means an automated agent checked each sentence against web-search excerpts and generated summaries of the cited sources on the date shown. It was not checked against the full texts, and not by a clinician or anatomist. A second automated review, with the same tools and limits, looked for errors; it is not peer review. The drawings are original schematics checked against these notes, not reviewed by an anatomist.

That second review was run on this dossier on 19 Oct 2026: 32 more searches (SR1–SR32), with the same tools and limits; it is not peer review and not a clinician or anatomist (§13).

**Gluteal muscles** (`hips.gluteals`)

- `hips.gluteals.function` (function, established_anatomy, reviewed): The gluteal muscles of the buttock are taught in two groups. A superficial group of large muscles, the gluteus maximus, medius and minimus with the tensor fasciae latae, extends the hip and moves the thigh out to the side; a deep group of smaller muscles, the piriformis among them, mainly turns the thigh outward. The gluteus medius also holds the pelvis steady when one leg is off the ground, and the gluteus minimus helps steady it in walking.

**Gluteus maximus** (`hips.gluteus_maximus`)

- `hips.gluteus_maximus.function` (function, established_anatomy, reviewed): The gluteus maximus extends the hip and turns the thigh outward (external rotation). Its upper fibres help move the thigh out to the side and its lower fibres help bring it in. Anatomy references describe it as the main hip extensor when force is needed, as in rising from a chair, straightening up from a bend, climbing and running. In a cadaver study of the hip's rotators it had a large capacity for turning the thigh outward, and a later paper citing that study notes that this capacity is reduced when the hip is bent. Through the iliotibial band it helps support the straightened knee.

  - kenhub.gluteus_maximus, Key facts: Action. Qualification: Seen through web-search excerpts of the page, not the full text.
  - teachmeanatomy.gluteal_region, Superficial muscles: gluteus maximus. Qualification: Seen through web-search excerpts of the page, not the full text.
  - pmid.10327003, Abstract. Qualification: Cadaver and computer-model study of rotation leverage (moment arms) in four specimens; abstract only. Two second-review searches read 'a large' capacity, not 'the largest'.

  - elsevier.gluteus_maximus, Actions: the extended knee. Qualification: Seen through a generated web-search summary of the page, not the page itself.

- `hips.gluteus_maximus.selectivity.1` (selectivity, experimental, reviewed): In a fine-wire EMG study of 20 healthy adults doing 11 common therapeutic exercises, the upper part of the gluteus maximus was more active, relative to its own maximum, than the lower part in the five exercises that included moving the thigh out to the side or turning it outward. **Gluteus medius** (`hips.gluteus_medius`)

- `hips.gluteus_medius.function` (function, established_anatomy, reviewed): The gluteus medius is a main hip abductor: the whole muscle moves the thigh out to the side, assisted by the gluteus minimus and the tensor fasciae latae. When one foot is off the ground, in standing, walking or running, it holds the pelvis steady over the standing leg. Its front fibres help turn the thigh inward, having the better leverage for it; one reference adds that its back fibres help turn the thigh outward when the knee is straight. How hip muscles turn the thigh can change as the hip bends: a cadaver study of the gluteal muscles and the other hip rotators found the pull shifting toward turning the thigh inward with hip flexion in most of the muscle parts it measured.
  - statpearls.gluteus_medius, Structure and Function. Qualification: Seen through web-search excerpts of the page, not the full text.
  - kenhub.gluteus_medius, Function. Qualification: Seen through web-search excerpts of the page, not the full text.
  - kenhub.gluteus_medius_video, Function: inward turning by the front fibres. Qualification: A video transcript seen through a search summary; the video was not watched.
  - pmid.10327003, Abstract. Qualification: Cadaver and computer-model study of four specimens; the abstract does not say which parts of each muscle changed.
- `hips.gluteus_medius.selectivity.1` (selectivity, experimental, reviewed): In a fine-wire EMG study of 20 healthy adults doing 11 exercises, the gluteus medius and the upper gluteus maximus were both more active than the tensor fasciae latae in bridging on one or both legs, hip extension on hands and knees, the clam, side-stepping and squatting. The clam favoured the two gluteal muscles over the tensor fasciae latae most, followed by side-stepping, the one-leg bridge and the hands-and-knees exercises. This is short-term muscle activity, not growth, and it does not show which exercise builds which muscle.
  - pmid.23160432, Abstract: Methods, Results and Conclusions. Qualification: Short-term muscle activity in a controlled task; not a measurement of growth and not a set-credit weight. Healthy adults, fine-wire electrodes; the figures come from secondary summaries of the abstract, which two searches agreed on.
- `hips.gluteus_medius.appearance` (appearance, anatomical_inference, reviewed): Only the back third of the gluteus medius lies under the gluteus maximus. Its larger front part, on the outer side of the hip, is covered by a thick sheet of fascia (the gluteal aponeurosis) rather than by muscle. It may therefore contribute to the contour of the upper outer hip beside the gluteus maximus, while its back third may add only to the fullness beneath that muscle. Visible shape also depends on neighbouring muscles, body fat and individual anatomy.
  - kenhub.gluteus_medius, Overview: what covers it. Qualification: Gives location and layer only; the contour statement is inferred from them, not reported by the source.
  - elsevier.gluteal_fascia, Description: the gluteal aponeurosis. Qualification: Gives location and layer only; the contour statement is inferred from them, not reported by the source.

**Gluteus minimus** (`hips.gluteus_minimus`)

- `hips.gluteus_minimus.function` (function, established_anatomy, reviewed): The gluteus minimus, the smallest of the gluteal muscles, lies just under the gluteus medius and is mainly a hip abductor and stabiliser: with the gluteus medius it moves the thigh out to the side and helps hold the pelvis steady in walking. Its front fibres help turn the thigh inward; one reference says this applies with the hip bent. In a cadaver and model study it could also act as a hip flexor, or turn the thigh either way, depending on the thigh's position and which part was working, and by tightening the hip joint capsule it helps hold the head of the thigh bone in its socket.
  - statpearls.gluteus_minimus, Introduction. Qualification: Introduction only; the rest of the article was behind a registration wall.
  - kenhub.gluteus_minimus, Function. Qualification: Seen through a generated web-search summary of the page, not the page itself.
  - kenhub.gluteus_minimus_video, Function: inward turning by the front fibres. Qualification: A video transcript seen through a search summary; the video was not watched.
  - boneandjoint.gluteus_minimus, Abstract. Qualification: 16 cadaver hips and a plastic-bone model; abstract only.
- `hips.gluteus_minimus.appearance` (appearance, anatomical_inference, reviewed): The gluteus minimus is the smallest and deepest of the gluteal muscles, lying under the gluteus medius on the outer surface of the pelvis, so it has little or no contour of its own; it may add to the general fullness of the side of the hip.
  - radiopaedia.gluteal_muscles, Gluteus minimus: the smallest and deepest. Qualification: Gives location and layer only; the contour statement is inferred from them, not reported by the source.

**Tensor fasciae latae** (`hips.tfl`)

- `hips.tfl.function` (function, established_anatomy, reviewed): The tensor fasciae latae works with the gluteal muscles in bending the hip, moving the thigh out to the side and turning it inward; anatomy references describe it as assisting in each, and one calls it a relatively weak abductor and inward rotator. In walking it helps balance the body's weight over the standing leg, raising the pelvis on the other side so the swinging leg clears the ground. It joins the iliotibial band, which carries its pull to the shin bone below the knee; references disagree about what it does at the knee.

- Surface visibility: **partial**. Just under the skin and easy to feel (kenhub.tensor_fasciae_latae), but small and enclosed between two layers of the fascia lata; the forearm marks superficial muscles whose outline is not wholly distinct 'partial'. 'clear' would also be defensible; the anatomy lane may prefer it.
- databaseMuscle: tensor_fasciae_latae. Exists in the canonical list (shared/capabilityRank.ts).
- Location: Outer side of the hip toward the front, just under the skin, about halfway between the bony point at the front of the pelvis and the bony point at the top of the thigh bone; it joins the iliotibial band, which runs down the outer thigh to below the knee.
- What it does: Helps bend your hip, move your thigh out to the side and turn it inward, working with the gluteal muscles, and helps keep the pelvis level when you walk. Through the iliotibial band it also reaches below the knee.
- Shape note: It lies just under the skin at the front and outer side of the hip, so it may contribute to the contour there. Visible shape also depends on neighbouring muscles, body fat and individual anatomy.
- Caveats: Anatomy references describe its hip actions as helping roles, alongside the gluteal muscles. / References disagree about what it does at the knee, so no knee action is counted for it here.

### Piriformis (`hips.piriformis`, Piriformis)

- Kind and role: muscle, adjacent; parent: none; node key: none; status: reviewed (Its function claim, hips:hips.piriformis.function, is reviewed.)
- Aliases: piriformis muscle
- Layer: deep. Supported: True; outcrop supported: None. Deep to the gluteus maximus (kenhub.piriformis); one of the deep group of the gluteal region (teachmeanatomy.gluteal_region).
- Surface visibility: **not_distinct**. Covered by the gluteus maximus (kenhub.piriformis).
- databaseMuscle: null. No canonical name for the piriformis in shared/capabilityRank.ts; the database lane may add one or leave it null.
- Location: Deep in the buttock, under the gluteus maximus: from the sacrum outward to the top of the bony point at the top of the thigh bone.
- What it does: Turns your thigh outward when the hip is straight, and helps move it out to the side when the hip is bent. It also helps hold the ball of the hip in its socket.
- Shape note: Deep, under the gluteus maximus, so it has little or no contour of its own. Visible shape also depends on neighbouring muscles, body fat and individual anatomy.
- Caveats: Shown beside the hip muscles because of where it sits; hip specialization does not target it. / Some references say that with the hip bent far its pull reverses and it turns the thigh inward; a study in living adults did not find that. No inward turning is counted for it here. / The sciatic nerve passes below it in most people; in some, part or all of the nerve passes through or above it.

### Iliopsoas (`hips.iliopsoas`, Iliopsoas)

- Kind and role: muscle, target; parent: none; node key: hipFlexors; status: reviewed (Its function claim, hips:hips.iliopsoas.function, is reviewed.)
- Aliases: iliacus and psoas major, psoas, hip flexor
- Layer: deep (surface outcrop, femoral triangle). Supported: True; outcrop supported: True. The psoas major arises from the lumbar vertebrae and the iliacus from the iliac fossa (statpearls.iliopsoas), both deep; below the groin the iliopsoas forms the outer part of the floor of the femoral triangle, whose roof is skin, superficial fascia and the fascia lata (teachmeanatomy.femoral_triangle). So the belly is deep with a near-surface window in the femoral triangle, as §1.2 says.
- Surface visibility: **not_distinct**. Deep in the abdomen and pelvis; only a short stretch lies under fascia and skin in the femoral triangle (teachmeanatomy.femoral_triangle), not as a separately visible outline.
- databaseMuscle: iliopsoas. Exists in the canonical list (shared/capabilityRank.ts).
- Location: Deep in the lower abdomen and at the front of the hip: the psoas major from the lumbar spine and the iliacus from the inner bowl of the pelvis end together on a small bony point near the top of the thigh bone. Just below the groin it forms the outer part of the floor of the femoral triangle, the hollow at the top of the front of the thigh.
- What it does: Bends your hip: it is the main muscle that brings the thigh forward and up. The psoas part also helps steady the lower spine.
- Shape note: Mostly deep inside the abdomen and pelvis, so it has little or no contour of its own; only a short stretch lies near the surface, in the hollow just below the groin. Visible shape also depends on neighbouring muscles, body fat and individual anatomy.
- Caveats: References disagree about whether, and which way, it turns the thigh, so no rotation is counted for it here. / Counted as one muscle here: the psoas major and the iliacus end together and both bend the hip, though in one study their activity differed in a leg raise.

### Adductor group (`hips.adductors`, Adductors)

- Kind and role: group, group; parent: none; node key: adductors; status: reviewed (Its function claim, hips:hips.adductors.function, is reviewed. The evidence speaks of the adductors as a group (kenhub.hip_adductors, statpearls.adductor_strain), so the parent has a function claim; it has no node actions.)
- Aliases: hip adductors, adductor muscles, inner thigh muscles
- Layer: — (§1.2: a parent, neither drawn nor selectable; the TypeScript value follows the platform lane, the forearm's groups use 'mixed'). Supported: True; outcrop supported: None. Members span the surface (adductor longus, pectineus, gracilis), the middle (adductor brevis) and the deepest front layer (adductor magnus) (kenhub.adductor_brevis_video, grays.medial_femoral_muscles); 'mixed' if the type needs one.
- Surface visibility: **partial**. Some members are at the surface of the inner thigh, others under them (kenhub.adductor_brevis_video, grays.medial_femoral_muscles, teachmeanatomy.femoral_triangle).
- databaseMuscle: null. The canonical list holds adductor_magnus, adductor_longus, adductor_brevis and gracilis separately, not the group.
- Location: Inner side of the thigh, from the front and lower pelvis around the pubic bone down the inner side of the thigh bone, in layers from the surface to deep.
- What it does: Bring your thigh in toward the other leg and help steady the pelvis. The adductor magnus also helps straighten the hip.
- Shape note: Together these muscles may make up most of the inner thigh's fullness. Which muscle shapes which part of that outline cannot be read from the surface. Visible shape also depends on neighbouring muscles, body fat and individual anatomy.
- Caveats: A group, not a muscle: work is counted for the muscles inside it. / References differ on whether the pectineus belongs to the group; here it and the gracilis are listed in it, beside the three adductors.

### Adductor magnus (`hips.adductor_magnus`, AddMag)

- Kind and role: muscle, target; parent: hips.adductors; node key: adductors; status: reviewed (Its function claim, hips:hips.adductor_magnus.function, is reviewed.)
- Aliases: adductor magnus muscle
- Layer: intermediate (surface outcrop, back view). Supported: True; outcrop supported: partly. From the front it lies behind the adductor brevis, which lies behind the pectineus and adductor longus (kenhub.adductor_brevis_video), so it is the deepest of the front's three layers; from behind, posterior dissection views of the most superficial muscles label it beside the semitendinosus and gracilis (anatomytool.ubc_posterior_lower_limb; stanford.bassett_182_6, with the fascia lata removed). That supports a back-view outcrop, seen only through image labels. One teaching page (learnmuscles, not cited) called it deep from both views. See deviation D1 on its layer relative to the adductor brevis. Second review: an independent search (SR22) found no written description of the back-view outcrop, and its summary called the muscle 'usually not visible' from behind (its own inference). Another (SR24) suggested a small front-view outcrop low on the inner thigh, from labelled images outside the standard's list. The back-view outcrop therefore rests on image labels alone; the anatomy lane should confirm it from an atlas plate before the drawing lane cuts it.
- Surface visibility: **partial**. Mostly covered by the other adductors and the hamstrings; part of it is labelled among the most superficial muscles in posterior views (anatomytool.ubc_posterior_lower_limb, stanford.bassett_182_6).
- databaseMuscle: adductor_magnus. Exists in the canonical list (shared/capabilityRank.ts).
- Location: Inner thigh, deepest of the adductors seen from the front: its adductor part runs from the pubic bone to the thigh bone, and its hamstring part from the sitting bone almost straight down to a bony point on the inner side of the thigh bone just above the knee. From behind, part of it lies on the inner side of the back of the thigh, beside the hamstrings and the gracilis.
- What it does: Brings your thigh in toward the other leg and straightens your hip: its hamstring part pulls the thigh back. It also helps steady the pelvis when you stand and walk.
- Shape note: Most of it lies under the other adductors and the hamstrings, so its own outline is only partly visible: it may contribute to the inner side of the back of the thigh and to the general fullness of the inner thigh. Visible shape also depends on neighbouring muscles, body fat and individual anatomy.
- Caveats: Its two parts are counted as one muscle here: the adductor part pulls the thigh in, the hamstring part also straightens the hip. / Its two parts are listed as turning the thigh in opposite directions, so no rotation is counted for it here.

### Adductor longus (`hips.adductor_longus`, AddLong)

- Location: Front of the inner thigh, at the surface: from the front of the pubic bone, near the groin, down and outward to the middle of the back of the thigh bone. By most accounts its inner edge is the inner border of the femoral triangle.
- What it does: Brings your thigh in toward the other leg and helps bend the hip.

### Pectineus (`hips.pectineus`, Pectineus)

### Gracilis (`hips.gracilis`, Gracilis)

## 6. Product questions (not evidence)

- TFL has no prime row on the evidence (all three of its hip actions are 'assists' or 'relatively weak'). regionContent.test.ts ('gives every muscle a main action, adjacent muscles aside') will fail for hips.tfl, a target muscle. Either the anatomy lane marks hip.abduction prime as a product choice and says so, or the test allows a target muscle with assist rows only. Not decided here. Second review (SR12): study flashcards built on a kinesiology textbook list the tensor fasciae latae among the primary hip flexors and primary hip abductors, and Kenhub calls it relatively weak. If the lane reads that textbook and it says so, a prime hip.flexion or hip.abduction row would rest on evidence rather than on a product choice; the flashcards themselves cannot be cited.
- No muscle of the hips is prime for hip.internal_rotation on the recorded evidence (gluteus medius, gluteus minimus and TFL are all assist), so the page never names inward rotation as a missing main action. If it should, marking the gluteus minimus's row prime is the closest product choice (see that row's productNote).
- The adductor magnus's hip.extension row is prime on the evidence; the thigh's external listing of the adductor magnus (§1.2) depends on it being prime.
- The thigh lane may want to know that the gracilis bends the knee and turns the lower leg inward (statpearls.gracilis, kenhub.gracilis); the gracilis's home is the hips and it is not listed by the thigh.
- The pectineus and the piriformis are adjacent; their prime rows do not drive suggestions (recommendations.ts reads prime rows of target muscles only).

## 9. Where sources disagree, and how the wording handles it

- Hip rotation and hip flexion. A cadaver and model study (pmid.10327003) found the pull of most hip muscle parts it measured shifting toward turning the thigh inward as the hip bends, but its abstract does not say which parts. Muscle by muscle the recorded evidence is: piriformis, outward with the hip straight and inward past about 60 degrees by one reference (others, uncited, say about 90), resting mainly on cadaver and model work, while a dynamic ultrasound study of 25 adults found no reversal (wcpt.piriformis_action; second review SR3, SR4); gluteus minimus, inward by its front fibres with the hip bent by one reference, either way depending on position and part in a cadaver model; gluteus medius, no muscle-specific evidence found (Q57), so its claim states only the general finding. The claims say this plainly and the node actions count only what holds with the hip straight, as help where the sources say help. Second review: the gluteus maximus's outward-turning capacity is reduced with the hip bent by a paper citing the 1999 study (pmc.damiano_hip_rotation), and an older thread-method study found the psoas major, pectineus, adductor longus and brevis turning the thigh either way depending on the hip's position (orthobullets.iliacus_rotation; SR21). A computer model (isb.adductor_longus_moment_arm) found the adductor longus turning it outward up to about 80 degrees of hip flexion and inward beyond. Each of those claims now says so; none of these rotations is counted.
- Adductor longus and hip flexion: a flexor up to about 90 degrees of hip flexion and an extensor beyond in one computer model (isb.adductor_longus_moment_arm, Pressel and Lengsfeld). The second review found no second source for the angle; an uncited summary put the adductors' change between about 40 and 70 degrees (SR8). Said in the claim and a caveat; counted as assist for flexion only.
- Adductor magnus and hip extension: Kenhub and StatPearls give extension to the hamstring part; StatPearls' adductor-strain article says the magnus 'contributes' to extension; an imaging study of 15 young adults estimated its extension capacity at more than twice its adduction capacity. Recorded prime on the first, second and fourth. The claim says what each found. Second review: the imaging estimate is for the hip in neutral (said in the claim now); a moment-arm source cited by a 2020 study gives the back part an extension moment (jbiomech.hip_adductor_stretch); how the extension leverage changes with hip flexion was seen only in secondary or uncited pages (SR8) and is not claimed.
- Rotation by the adductors: adductor longus outward (Kenhub) versus some inward (StatPearls); adductor brevis outward (Kenhub) versus inward (Radiopaedia), with a Kenhub video calling it contested; pectineus possibly either way (Kenhub); and for the longus, brevis and pectineus an older study found the direction set by the hip's position (second review, SR21); adductor magnus parts listed in opposite directions (Kenhub). None counted; each claim says references disagree.
- Iliopsoas rotation: StatPearls and Kenhub list it as helping turn the thigh outward; an older thread-method abstract reports the iliacus turning the thigh inward in every position tested and the psoas major either way by position (SR21). Not counted; the claim says the references do not agree.
- Tensor fasciae latae at the knee: StatPearls an accessory knee flexor beyond 30 degrees with outward turning of the shin; Kenhub knee extension and outward turning. Not counted; the claim and a caveat say references disagree. Its hip actions are 'assists' in StatPearls and Elsevier and 'relatively weak' in Kenhub, which is why all its rows are assist.
- Gluteus medius back fibres: StatPearls ties their outward turning to the knee being straight; Kenhub does not list outward turning at all. The claim says 'one reference adds … when the knee is straight' (the condition added by the second review).
- Gluteus maximus and buttock shape: Wikipedia (read, not cited) says fat contributes much of the buttock's rounded shape; the shape notes carry the forearm's sentence that visible shape also depends on body fat, and no sentence says the muscle alone makes the outline.
- Psoas minor: absent rates differ widely between studies and populations, and the two cited studies disagree on whether race matters. The variation claim states both without a figure.
- Pectineus nerve supply: Kenhub gives the femoral nerve with the obturator as a variant; Radiopaedia splits the muscle between the femoral and accessory obturator nerves. The claim says 'described as mixed' and 'may supply'.
- Pectineus nerve supply (second review): an independent search (SR26) found the femoral nerve the only supply in most people and an obturator or accessory obturator branch in a minority; the claim now says the femoral nerve supplies it and that the second supply is described by one reference and listed as a variant by another.
- Adductor magnus back-view outcrop (second review): an independent search (SR22) found no written description of it; the image-label evidence is unchanged and the appearance claim now says only what the labels show.

## 10. Deviations from §1.2

None changes an id, a short name or a role; the adversarial review decides whether either changes a layer or a parent.

**D1. layer: hips.adductor_magnus (intermediate) and hips.adductor_brevis (deep).** Fixed in §1.2: adductor magnus 'intermediate (surface outcrop, back view)'; adductor brevis 'deep'. Evidence: Seen from the front, the adductor brevis lies deep to the pectineus and adductor longus but superficial to the adductor magnus (kenhub.adductor_brevis_video, verbatim: 'the adductor brevis muscle can be found deep to the pectineus and adductor longus muscles but superficial to the adductor magnus muscle'; lecturio.medial_thigh_muscles). §1.2's layers put the magnus above the brevis. Assessment: Not a contradiction for the back view, where the magnus lies under the hamstrings with part of it at the surface and the brevis is not seen, nor for a Front view drawn with only surface and deep layers (§7.2) if the magnus is not drawn there. It is a contradiction if the drawing shows the magnus on the Front at all: anatomically it lies behind the brevis from the front. Second review: the front-view order (longus and pectineus, then brevis, then magnus) is confirmed by a dissection walkthrough ('having cut adductor longus, see adductor magnus', SR24). The same search suggested that a small part of the magnus shows low on the inner thigh in front views, medial to the vastus medialis and below the adductor longus. That came from labelled images outside the standard's list. If an atlas plate confirms it, the magnus has a front-view outcrop too, and the drawing lane would draw only that strip in the Front view. The back-view outcrop rests on image labels alone (SR22). Proposal: Keep §1.2's ids and layers; the drawing lane does not draw the adductor magnus in the Front view (or draws it on Front-deep only where the brevis does not cover it, which would need a Front-middle layer for the brevis). The adversarial review decides; this lane does not change §1.2. Second review: agree with keeping §1.2's ids and layers; the anatomy lane should confirm both outcrops (back view, and the possible low front strip) from an atlas plate before the drawing lane cuts them.

**D2. parent: hips.tfl (none in §1.2).** Fixed in §1.2: tensor fasciae latae has no parent; the gluteals' children are the gluteus maximus, medius and minimus. Evidence: Kenhub's gluteal muscles article and TeachMeAnatomy's gluteal region page both count the tensor fasciae latae among the superficial gluteal muscles (kenhub.gluteal_muscles; teachmeanatomy.gluteal_region). Assessment: A grouping choice, not an anatomical error: §1.2 gives the TFL its own body-map key (tfl) and keeps it outside the gluteals. The gluteals' function claim names the TFL as a member of the superficial gluteal group in the references, and the gluteals' caveat says it is listed on its own here. Proposal: No change. Recorded so the review sees the wording choice.
