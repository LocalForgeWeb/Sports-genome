# Specialization atlas: shoulder anatomy and its evidence (19 Oct 2026)

What the shoulders' anatomy content says, what it rests on, and how far that review can be trusted. This is the anatomy lane's research dossier for wave B; the content files it feeds are `shared/specialization/regions/shoulders/{nodes,nodeActions,claims}.ts`, `shared/specialization/sources/shoulders.ts` and, for any action wave 0 has not already added, `shared/specialization/actions/shoulders.ts`. Ids, names, short names, kinds, roles, layers, parents and region actions are those fixed in 10-multi-region.md §1.2; the method, limits and wording follow 03-anatomy-evidence.md. The machine-readable form, with every query and excerpt, is `dossier.json` beside this file.

## 1. Method

**What was reviewed.** Every node of the region in §1.2: the deltoid and the rotator cuff (groups), the three deltoid heads, and the four rotator cuff muscles; 9 nodes. For each, a function and an appearance claim; selectivity claims where a citable EMG or clinical finding exists; one variation claim. 26 claims, 12 node-action rows and 8 joint actions. The six external members (two pectoral heads, serratus anterior, upper trapezius, latissimus dorsi, teres major) were not researched; their home lanes own them.

**How.** Web search only, as for the forearm. Page fetches are refused by the network proxy, so each claim was checked against what the search results showed: titles, URLs and the tool's generated summaries with quoted excerpts. 54 searches were run, batched by muscle and by question, several restricted to one publisher's domain so that an excerpt could be tied to its page. For each claim:

1. Search for reputable references in the evidence standard's order: NCBI Bookshelf (StatPearls), peer-reviewed papers, university anatomy pages (Loyola University Chicago's dissector), Kenhub, Elsevier Complete Anatomy, Radiopaedia, IMAIOS. Wikipedia was read and never cited. Physiopedia, a professional wiki outside that list, is cited twice, each time only beside an Elsevier reference for the same action, and says so in its qualification.
2. Add each relied-on reference to the region's sources with a `<publisher>.<topic>` or `pmid.<number>` key and a note saying exactly what the search showed and what it could not confirm.
3. Cite it on the claim with a locator (the section the excerpt came from) and a qualification where the source is limited (search excerpt, abstract only, acute EMG, a testing study, location only).
4. Record, for every citation, the query run and the excerpt relied on, verbatim (`citations` in the JSON).
5. Mark a claim `reviewed` only when the recorded excerpts support every sentence, with `reviewedAt: "2026-10-19"` and the note "Checked against the cited sources through web search on 19 Oct 2026; automated review, not a clinician or anatomist." Appearance claims carry the forearm's appearance note adapted to the date, are always `anatomical_inference`, and are never a measured or predicted change in size.
6. A node is `reviewed` only when all its function claims are.

Wording was then cut back until each sentence said no more than its excerpts. Where references disagreed, the claim says what each says, or the point is left out (§6).

## 2. Limits

- **Excerpts, not full texts.** The StatPearls articles showed their introductions and parts of "Structure and Function"; the infraspinatus and deltoid point-of-care copies are behind a login. Every EMG and cadaver study was seen as an abstract or a summary of one. Each citation says so in its qualification.
- **Search summaries can misquote.** The tool's summaries are generated. One summary (query Q25) said the posterior deltoid's outward rotation was found only on a flashcard site, while Elsevier, Loyola and Kenhub each list it in their own excerpts; another (Coratella 2020) reported a results line and a conclusion line that read against each other. Function claims rest on two or more independent references, and each selectivity claim states only the lines seen twice or quoted directly.
- **Automated review.** "Reviewed" means checked by this automated process against search results. It is not a review by a clinician or an anatomist. A human anatomy review is the next step before publishing the shoulders' version as reviewed content.
- **Nothing here is hypertrophy evidence.** The selectivity claims cite acute EMG studies, several of them clinical testing positions in healthy volunteers. Each says what was measured and that it is not growth, and none may become a set-credit weight.
- **Percentages are kept out of athlete copy.** Where a study reported activity as a share of a maximal contraction, the claims say which exercise was higher, not by how much.

### The statements

- `shoulders.deltoid.function`: The deltoid's three parts act differently. The front (clavicular) part raises the arm forward and turns it inward, the outer (acromial) part raises it out to the side, and the back (spinal) part moves it backward and turns it outward. The front and back parts also help steady the shoulder while the arm is raised to the side.
- `shoulders.deltoid.appearance`: The deltoid is a thick triangular muscle lying just under the skin over the front, outer side and back of the shoulder joint, so it may contribute most of the shoulder's rounded contour. Visible shape also depends on neighbouring muscles, body fat and individual anatomy.
- `shoulders.deltoid.variation.1`: The deltoid is usually taught as three parts, but its inner structure is finer and differs between people. A study of 60 cadaver shoulders divided it into seven segments by its internal tendons, and a study of 44 deltoids divided by their nerve branches found between five and eight main segments, seven in half of them. The three parts used here are a simplification.
- `shoulders.anterior_deltoid.function`: The front (clavicular) part of the deltoid raises the arm forward (flexion) and turns it inward (internal rotation); anatomy references name it, with the upper part of the pectoralis major, as a main forward raiser of the arm. It also helps bring a raised arm forward across the chest (horizontal adduction) and helps steady the shoulder. Moment-arm studies in cadavers rank it, with the outer part, among the most effective lifters of the arm out to the side.
- `shoulders.anterior_deltoid.selectivity.1`: How hard the front of the deltoid works depends on the exercise. In ten competitive bodybuilders it was more active in a front raise than in four kinds of lateral raise; in 13 trained men it was more active in the shoulder press than in the bench press, the dumbbell fly or the lateral raise. Both are short-term surface-EMG findings; neither study measured growth.
- `shoulders.anterior_deltoid.selectivity.2`: In a surface-EMG study of 30 trained adults bench pressing at five bench angles, from flat to 60 degrees, front-deltoid activity was highest at the steepest angle, and the authors concluded that inclines above 45 degrees raise front-deltoid activity and reduce the pectoralis major's contribution. Bench angle therefore belongs in how a press is described; the study did not measure growth.
- `shoulders.anterior_deltoid.appearance`: The front part of the deltoid lies just under the skin at the front of the shoulder, from the outer third of the collarbone, and a groove (the deltopectoral groove) separates it from the pectoralis major, so it may contribute to the front of the shoulder's rounded contour beside the upper chest.
- `shoulders.lateral_deltoid.function`: The outer (acromial) part of the deltoid is the main muscle raising the arm out to the side (abduction). Anatomy references describe it doing most of the lifting once the movement has started, with the supraspinatus needed to start it from the arm hanging at the side.
- `shoulders.lateral_deltoid.selectivity.1`: In ten competitive bodybuilders, the outer deltoid was more active in a lateral raise with the arm in a neutral position than in the same raise with the arm turned out, with the elbow bent, or in a front raise. In 13 trained men it was more active in the lateral raise and the shoulder press than in the bench press or the dumbbell fly. Both are short-term surface-EMG findings, not measurements of growth.
- `shoulders.lateral_deltoid.appearance`: The outer part of the deltoid runs from the outer edge of the bony tip of the shoulder (the acromion) down the outer side of the upper arm, just under the skin, so it may contribute to the outer curve of the shoulder.
- `shoulders.posterior_deltoid.function`: The back (spinal) part of the deltoid moves the arm backward (extension), turns it outward (external rotation) and moves a raised arm backward (horizontal abduction). It also helps steady the shoulder while the arm is raised to the side.
- `shoulders.posterior_deltoid.selectivity.1`: In a surface-EMG study of 12 trained men, the back of the deltoid was more active in a reverse fly machine, which moves the raised arm backward, than in a seated row or an inclined pulldown. In ten competitive bodybuilders doing lateral and front raises, it was most active in a lateral raise with the arm turned inward. These are short-term activity findings; neither study measured growth.
- `shoulders.posterior_deltoid.appearance`: The back part of the deltoid runs from the bony ridge of the shoulder blade (its spine) to the outer side of the upper arm, just under the skin, so it may contribute to the back of the shoulder's rounded contour. It lies over parts of the infraspinatus and teres minor.
- `shoulders.rotator_cuff.function`: The rotator cuff is four muscles, the supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis, that run from the shoulder blade to the upper-arm bone. Working together they press the head of the bone into its shallow socket and keep it from sliding while the arm moves; separately they turn the arm outward (infraspinatus and teres minor) or inward (subscapularis) and help raise it to the side (supraspinatus).
- `shoulders.rotator_cuff.appearance`: The rotator cuff lies mostly under the deltoid and the trapezius, and the subscapularis lies on the rib-facing side of the shoulder blade, so as a group it has little contour of its own. Only the infraspinatus and teres minor, on the back of the shoulder blade, are described as partly rather than wholly covered by other muscles.
- `shoulders.supraspinatus.function`: Supraspinatus helps raise the arm out to the side (abduction), working with the deltoid; anatomy references describe it as starting the movement from the arm hanging at the side. With the other three rotator cuff muscles it helps hold the head of the upper-arm bone in its shallow socket.
- `shoulders.supraspinatus.selectivity.1`: The supraspinatus works together with the deltoid whenever the arm is raised to the side. In two EMG studies of healthy adults (10 and 21 people), its activity relative to the deltoid was highest with the arm raised only about 30 degrees, slightly forward and turned out, and lowest, or nearly so, in the 'empty can' position used in clinical testing. These are short-term findings from testing positions, not exercises, and not measurements of growth.
- `shoulders.supraspinatus.appearance`: Supraspinatus fills the hollow above the bony ridge of the shoulder blade, under the trapezius, and its tendon passes under the bony tip of the shoulder, so it has little or no visible contour of its own.
- `shoulders.infraspinatus.function`: Infraspinatus turns the arm outward (external rotation), working with the teres minor. As part of the rotator cuff it presses the head of the upper-arm bone into its socket, balancing the pull of the subscapularis at the front.
- `shoulders.infraspinatus.selectivity.1`: Turning the arm outward works the infraspinatus together with the back of the deltoid, and their shares change with position. In a surface-EMG study of 32 people with rounded shoulders, infraspinatus activity was highest in an exercise position the authors based on the muscle's architecture, while the side-lying version gave the highest ratio of infraspinatus to rear-deltoid activity. In another study, watching the EMG signal during side-lying external rotation lowered rear-deltoid activity and raised infraspinatus activity. Neither study measured growth.
- `shoulders.infraspinatus.appearance`: Infraspinatus is a thick triangular muscle covering most of the back of the shoulder blade below its ridge. It lies under the trapezius and parts of the deltoid and the latissimus dorsi, so it has little separately visible contour of its own; where it is not covered, it may contribute to the contour on the back of the shoulder blade.
- `shoulders.teres_minor.function`: Teres minor turns the arm outward (external rotation), working with the infraspinatus, and as part of the rotator cuff helps hold the head of the upper-arm bone in its socket. Some references also list a role in bringing the arm toward the body.
- `shoulders.teres_minor.appearance`: Teres minor is a narrow muscle along the outer edge of the shoulder blade, just below the infraspinatus and above the teres major, and the back of the deltoid covers its upper outer part, so it has little separately visible contour of its own.
- `shoulders.subscapularis.function`: Subscapularis is the only rotator cuff muscle that turns the arm inward (internal rotation), and the largest and strongest of the four. It also helps hold the head of the upper-arm bone in its socket and can help bring the arm toward the body.
- `shoulders.subscapularis.selectivity.1`: The upper and lower parts of the subscapularis can be recorded separately, and arm position changes which works harder. In 15 healthy people doing seven shoulder exercises, the upper part was more active than the lower in every exercise except turning the arm inward with the arm at the side, and the push-up plus and a diagonal exercise activated both parts most. In a study of two clinical tests in 16 people, one test favoured the upper part and the other the lower. These are short-term activity findings, not growth.
- `shoulders.subscapularis.appearance`: Subscapularis covers the front, rib-facing surface of the shoulder blade, and its tendon crosses the front of the shoulder joint, so it lies under other structures and has little or no visible contour of its own.

## 4. Nodes

### Deltoid (`shoulders.deltoid`, Deltoid, muscle, group)

- **Layer (§1.2):** —. Parent node, not drawn (layer '—' in §1.2). All three parts are surface muscles, so 'surface' is the honest value if the type needs one.
- **Layer value for the type:** `surface` (suggested; §1.2 gives '—' for parents).
- **Surface visibility:** `clear`. Superficial over the whole shoulder; references call it easily observed and palpated and say it gives the shoulder its rounded contour (kenhub.deltoid, elsevier.deltoid, statpearls.deltoid).
- **Catalog key:** `shoulders` · **databaseMuscle (candidate):** none
- **Aliases:** deltoid muscle, delts, deltoids
- **Location:** Caps the shoulder at the front, outer side and back, from the collarbone and the shoulder blade down to the outer side of the upper arm.
- **What it does:** Its front part raises your arm forward and turns it inward, its outer part raises the arm out to the side, and its back part moves the arm backward and turns it outward. It also helps hold the shoulder steady.
- **Shape note:** Together its three parts make up most of the rounded outline of the shoulder. Visible shape also depends on neighbouring muscles, body fat and individual anatomy.
- **Caveat:** A group, not a muscle: work is counted for its three parts.
- **Caveat:** The three parts are a teaching simplification: anatomy studies divide the deltoid into more segments, five to eight in one study of its nerve branches, and the count differs between people.

### Anterior deltoid (`shoulders.anterior_deltoid`, AntDelt, head, target)

- **Layer (§1.2):** surface. Surface: supported (kenhub.deltoid, elsevier.deltoid).
- **Surface visibility:** `clear`. Just under the skin at the front of the shoulder and bounded from the chest by the deltopectoral groove (kenhub.deltoid, radiopaedia.deltopectoral_groove).
- **Catalog key:** `frontDelts` · **databaseMuscle (candidate):** `anterior_deltoid`
- **Aliases:** front delt, front deltoid, anterior delt, clavicular deltoid
- **Location:** Front of the shoulder, from the outer third of the collarbone down to the outer side of the upper arm.
- **What it does:** Raises your arm forward and turns it inward. It also helps bring a raised arm across the front of the chest and helps raise the arm out to the side.
- **Shape note:** May contribute to the front of the rounded shoulder outline, beside the upper chest, where a groove separates it from the pectoralis major. Visible shape also depends on neighbouring muscles, body fat and individual anatomy.
- **Caveat:** Presses and front raises load it together with the upper chest; how the work splits between them changes with the exercise and the bench angle.

### Lateral deltoid (`shoulders.lateral_deltoid`, LatDelt, head, target)

- **Layer (§1.2):** surface. Surface: supported (kenhub.deltoid, elsevier.deltoid).
- **Surface visibility:** `clear`. Just under the skin on the outer side of the shoulder (kenhub.deltoid, elsevier.deltoid_acromial).
- **Catalog key:** `sideDelts` · **databaseMuscle (candidate):** `middle_deltoid`
- **Aliases:** side delt, middle deltoid, middle delt, acromial deltoid
- **Location:** Outer side of the shoulder, from the outer edge of the bony tip of the shoulder (the acromion) down to the outer side of the upper arm.
- **What it does:** Raises your arm out to the side. It does most of the lifting once the movement has started; the supraspinatus helps start it.
- **Shape note:** May contribute to the outer curve of the shoulder. Visible shape also depends on neighbouring muscles, body fat and individual anatomy.
- **Caveat:** Studies call this part the middle, lateral or medial deltoid; all three names mean the same part.
- **Caveat:** Raising the arm out to the side also works the supraspinatus, which lies under the trapezius and the bony tip of the shoulder.

### Posterior deltoid (`shoulders.posterior_deltoid`, PostDelt, head, target)

- **Layer (§1.2):** surface. Surface: supported (kenhub.deltoid, elsevier.deltoid).
- **Surface visibility:** `clear`. Just under the skin at the back of the shoulder; it covers parts of the infraspinatus and teres minor rather than being covered (kenhub.deltoid, kenhub.infraspinatus, kenhub.teres_minor).
- **Catalog key:** `rearDelts` · **databaseMuscle (candidate):** `posterior_deltoid`
- **Aliases:** rear delt, rear deltoid, posterior delt, spinal deltoid
- **Location:** Back of the shoulder, from the bony ridge of the shoulder blade (its spine) down to the outer side of the upper arm.
- **What it does:** Moves a raised arm backward, brings the arm back and down from in front of the body, and turns the arm outward.
- **Shape note:** May contribute to the back of the rounded shoulder outline, over parts of the infraspinatus and teres minor. Visible shape also depends on neighbouring muscles, body fat and individual anatomy.
- **Caveat:** In one comparison it worked harder in a reverse fly than in a seated row or a pulldown.
- **Caveat:** Turning the arm outward loads it together with the infraspinatus and teres minor.

### Rotator cuff (`shoulders.rotator_cuff`, RotCuff, group, group)

- **Layer (§1.2):** —. Parent node, not drawn (layer '—' in §1.2). All four members are 'deep' in §1.2, so 'deep' is the honest value if the type needs one.
- **Layer value for the type:** `deep` (suggested; §1.2 gives '—' for parents).
- **Surface visibility:** `partial`. Supraspinatus and subscapularis are covered; parts of the infraspinatus and teres minor are not covered by other muscles (kenhub.infraspinatus, kenhub.teres_minor).
- **Catalog key:** `rotatorCuff` · **databaseMuscle (candidate):** none
- **Aliases:** rotator cuff muscles, SITS muscles, rotator cuff group
- **Location:** Four muscles from the shoulder blade to the top of the upper-arm bone, wrapping the shoulder joint at the front, the top and the back.
- **What it does:** Holds the ball of the upper-arm bone in its shallow socket while the arm moves, and turns the arm inward and outward.
- **Shape note:** Mostly deep, under the deltoid and the trapezius, so as a group it has little or no contour of its own; only parts of the infraspinatus and teres minor reach the surface, on the back of the shoulder blade. Visible shape also depends on neighbouring muscles, body fat and individual anatomy.
- **Caveat:** A group, not a muscle: work is counted for the four muscles inside it.

### Supraspinatus (`shoulders.supraspinatus`, SSP, muscle, target)

- **Layer (§1.2):** deep. Deep: supported (under the trapezius; kenhub.supraspinatus). No outcrop claimed, none found.
- **Surface visibility:** `not_distinct`. Covered by the trapezius, with its tendon under the acromion (kenhub.supraspinatus, statpearls.supraspinatus).
- **Catalog key:** `rotatorCuff` · **databaseMuscle (candidate):** `supraspinatus`
- **Aliases:** supraspinatus muscle
- **Location:** Top of the shoulder blade, above its bony ridge and under the trapezius; its tendon runs under the bony tip of the shoulder to the top of the upper-arm bone.
- **What it does:** Helps raise your arm out to the side, working with the deltoid; anatomy texts describe it as starting the movement. It also helps hold the ball of the upper-arm bone in its socket.
- **Shape note:** Lies under the trapezius and the bony tip of the shoulder, so it has little or no visible contour of its own.
- **Caveat:** Raising the arm to the side works it together with the deltoid; testing studies found positions that favour it, not ones that isolate it.
- **Caveat:** Anatomy texts stress its role at the start of the movement; it also aids the deltoid after that, and the deltoid does most of the lifting.

### Infraspinatus (`shoulders.infraspinatus`, ISP, muscle, target)

- **Layer (§1.2):** deep (surface outcrop, back view). Deep with a back-view surface outcrop: supported by kenhub.infraspinatus ('deep to trapezius, and parts of the deltoid and latissimus dorsi'). elsevier.infraspinatus says deep to the trapezius and deltoid without saying 'parts of'; not a contradiction of the outcrop, but less specific.
- **Surface visibility:** `partial`. Kenhub places it deep to the trapezius and only parts of the deltoid and latissimus dorsi, which leaves part of it uncovered on the back of the shoulder blade (kenhub.infraspinatus).
- **Catalog key:** `rotatorCuff` · **databaseMuscle (candidate):** `infraspinatus`
- **Aliases:** infraspinatus muscle
- **Location:** Back of the shoulder blade, below its bony ridge; its tendon crosses the back of the shoulder joint to the top of the upper-arm bone.
- **What it does:** Turns your arm outward, and helps hold the ball of the upper-arm bone in its socket.
- **Shape note:** Covers most of the back of the shoulder blade below its ridge. Much of it lies under the trapezius, the back of the deltoid and the latissimus dorsi, so it has little separately visible contour of its own; where it is not covered it may contribute to the contour on the back of the shoulder blade. Visible shape also depends on neighbouring muscles, body fat and individual anatomy.
- **Caveat:** Turning the arm outward works it together with the teres minor and the back of the deltoid; how the work splits between them changes with arm position and is not known exactly.

### Teres minor (`shoulders.teres_minor`, TMinor, muscle, target)

- **Layer (§1.2):** deep (surface outcrop, back view). Deep with a back-view surface outcrop: supported by kenhub.teres_minor (the deltoid covers its superolateral aspect). statpearls.teres_minor calls it 'located deep to the deltoid' without qualification; the two read differently, and the outcrop rests on Kenhub. §7.2 already allows it to be list-only if it cannot hold a 44 px square.
- **Surface visibility:** `partial`. Kenhub says the deltoid covers its upper outer part, leaving the rest near the surface; it is narrow and lies between the infraspinatus and teres major (kenhub.teres_minor, statpearls.teres_minor).
- **Catalog key:** `rotatorCuff` · **databaseMuscle (candidate):** `teres_minor`
- **Aliases:** teres minor muscle
- **Location:** Outer edge of the shoulder blade on the back, just below the infraspinatus and above the teres major; its tendon reaches the top of the upper-arm bone from behind.
- **What it does:** Turns your arm outward, alongside the infraspinatus, and helps hold the ball of the upper-arm bone in its socket.
- **Shape note:** Narrow, and partly covered by the back of the deltoid, so it has little separately visible contour of its own; at the outer edge of the shoulder blade it is hard to tell apart from the infraspinatus and the teres major. Visible shape also depends on neighbouring muscles, body fat and individual anatomy.
- **Caveat:** It turns the arm outward together with the infraspinatus, so outward-turning work loads both.
- **Caveat:** Not the teres major, the muscle just below it, which belongs to the back.

### Subscapularis (`shoulders.subscapularis`, SSC, muscle, target)

- **Layer (§1.2):** deep. Deep: supported. Drawing note (not a §1.2 change): from the front only its tendon and outer part cross in front of the joint (statpearls.subscapularis); the belly lies behind the rib cage in a front view.
- **Surface visibility:** `not_distinct`. On the costal surface of the scapula, behind the serratus anterior and the shoulder joint (elsevier.subscapularis, kenhub.subscapularis).
- **Catalog key:** `rotatorCuff` · **databaseMuscle (candidate):** `subscapularis`
- **Aliases:** subscapularis muscle
- **Location:** Front, rib-facing surface of the shoulder blade; its tendon crosses the front of the shoulder joint to the upper-arm bone.
- **What it does:** Turns your arm inward, and helps hold the ball of the upper-arm bone in its socket. It is the largest muscle of the rotator cuff.
- **Shape note:** Lies on the rib-facing side of the shoulder blade, under other structures, so it has little or no visible contour of its own.
- **Caveat:** Its upper and lower parts can be recorded separately, and in studies arm position changed which part worked harder.

## 6. What was deliberately not claimed, and disagreements

**Draft claims: none.** Where support was missing the content was left out rather than kept as a draft, as for the forearm:

- Horizontal abduction for the infraspinatus and teres minor: Elsevier lists it ('transversely abducts') and nothing else seen does; left out of node actions, as the forearm left out minor actions.
- Weak external rotation by the supraspinatus (StatPearls 'possible weak contribution'): left out.
- Adduction by the teres minor and subscapularis: said in their function claims, but shoulder.adduction is not a shoulders region action (it belongs to the chest and back).
- Horizontal abduction by the lateral deltoid (Physiopedia; Botton found more middle-deltoid activity in the reverse peck deck than the pulldown): not claimed.
- Variation claims for the rotator cuff: infraspinatus and teres minor fusion and accessory subscapularis slips were found only as single-specimen case reports (Wikipedia read, not cited).
- A teres minor selectivity claim: no study recording it on its own was seen with results (Reinold 2004's results were not shown; a physiotherapy blog's account was not used).
- Trebs 2010 (incline bench): seen only secondhand; not cited.
- IMAIOS deltoid: its lines were merged with other sites in the summary, so they were not attributed or cited.
- No clinical content: tear rates and testing accuracy are not stated as claims; the testing studies are cited only for what they measured in healthy people.

**Where references disagree, and how the wording handles it:**

- Starting abduction. StatPearls (deltoid) says the deltoid cannot start abduction and Elsevier (acromial part) says the supraspinatus is needed to start it; Kenhub says the supraspinatus is essential for the early range. No source seen tested whether the supraspinatus matters beyond the start. Wording: the supraspinatus 'helps raise the arm' and references 'describe it as starting the movement'; its caveat says references differ on its role beyond the start.
- Teres minor cover. StatPearls: 'located deep to the deltoid'; Kenhub: the deltoid covers its superolateral aspect. The appearance claim says the deltoid covers its upper outer part (Kenhub) and the outcrop in §1.2 rests on Kenhub.
- Infraspinatus as a rotator. A 2024 case report's introduction (PMC11013571, 'Novel Muscle in Infraspinous Fossa') says it makes only a minor contribution to lateral rotation and mainly stabilises; StatPearls, Elsevier and Kenhub list external rotation as its action. Not cited; the claim keeps the reference texts' action and also states the stabilising role.
- Coratella 2020. The results lines say the medial deltoid was more active in the neutral lateral raise than the externally rotated one; the conclusion line says external rotation increases anterior and medial deltoid activation (probably against internal rotation). The lateral-deltoid selectivity claim uses only the results lines.
- Rodriguez-Ridao 2020. The abstract's overall conclusion says the flat bench gives similar pectoralis and anterior deltoid activity, while the conclusion quoted from the full text says inclines above 45 degrees raise anterior deltoid activity. The claim states the incline finding and the authors' conclusion only.
- Anterior deltoid at low abduction angles. Physiopedia says the clavicular and spinal parts adduct below about 60 degrees and abduct above it; the moment-arm studies rank the anterior part among the most effective abductors. The anterior deltoid's abduction row is 'assist', and no abduction row is given to the posterior part.
- Subscapularis tests. Tokish 2003 found the belly-press and lift-off tests favoured different parts; a later study (Pennock 2011, not cited) found three tests did not differ. The claim reports Tokish as one study.
- A generated summary (Q25) said the posterior deltoid's external rotation was found only on a flashcard site, while Elsevier, Loyola and Kenhub each list it. The specific references are cited; this is an example of the summaries' limits.
