## 1. Method

**What was reviewed.** Every node of the lower leg in §1.2: the calf (triceps surae, a parent) with the gastrocnemius (one node for both heads) and the soleus; the tibialis anterior; the fibularis longus and brevis as one node; and the tibialis posterior (adjacent). The region has no external members. For each node, a function and an appearance claim, plus selectivity claims where a citable EMG finding exists (three) and variation claims where a real difference between people was found (four).

**How.** Web search only, as for the forearm. Page fetches are refused by the network proxy, so each claim was checked against what the search results showed: titles, URLs and the tool's generated summaries with quoted excerpts. 67 searches were logged (Q1 to Q67 in `searchlog.md`; Q1 to Q3 come from a first attempt of this lane that stopped early), batched by muscle and by question. For each claim:

1. Search for reputable references in the evidence standard's order: NCBI Bookshelf (StatPearls), peer-reviewed papers, university and teaching pages (TeachMeAnatomy, a Keimyung University repository record, the 1918 Gray's Anatomy as quoted on a third-party page), Kenhub, Elsevier Complete Anatomy, Radiopaedia. IMAIOS appeared only once, in a summary line not tied to its page, and is not cited. Wikipedia, WikiMSK, Physiopedia, DocCheck Flexikon and coaching sites appeared and were read where shown; none is cited.
2. Add each relied-on reference with a `<publisher>.<topic>` key, or `pmid.<number>` where the PubMed record itself was shown, and a note saying exactly what the search showed and what it could not confirm.
3. Cite it on the claim with a locator (the section the excerpt came from) and a qualification where the source is limited (search excerpt or summary, location only, abstract only, acute EMG, a video transcript, a cadaver leverage study).
4. Record, for every citation, the query and the excerpt relied on, verbatim from the search output (`citations` in the JSON; fragments of one result are joined with ' ... '). The build script refuses an excerpt fragment that is not word for word in `searchlog.md` and a query number that is not logged.
5. Mark a claim `reviewed` only when the recorded excerpts support every sentence, with `reviewedAt: "2026-10-19"` and the note "Checked against the cited sources through web search on 19 Oct 2026; automated review, not a clinician or anatomist." Appearance claims carry the forearm's appearance note adapted to the date and are always `anatomical_inference`.
6. A node is `reviewed` only when all its function claims are. The calf (a parent) has a function claim only because the references speak of the calf muscles as a group (StatPearls' calf article, Radiopaedia's triceps surae, the moment-arm study's 'triceps surae'); it has no node actions.

Wording was rewritten until each sentence said no more than its excerpts. Where references disagree (the tibialis anterior's share in inversion, the fibularis muscles and the tibialis posterior in plantar flexion, which gastrocnemius head works more with the toes turned), the claim says that they disagree. The build script also checks the copy rules of `regionContent.test.ts` and `forearmAnatomy.test.ts` over every node field, caveat, statement, locator and qualification: no per-cent sign or 'per cent', no growth promise, no 'optimal' or 'guarantee', no vendor name, no 'table', no 'left' or 'right' (so 'bent to 90 degrees', never 'a right angle'); deep muscles say 'little or no' contour; surface target muscles' shape notes are qualified; names, short names and aliases are unique; the four actions' wording equals `joints.ts`.

**Dates.** The searches ran on 20 Oct 2026; review and retrieval dates are written as 19 Oct 2026, the date fixed for this wave's review. If the lane prefers the run date, every `reviewedAt`, `retrievedOn` and review note changes together.

## 2. Limits

- **Excerpts and summaries, not full texts.** StatPearls showed introductions and parts of Structure and Function; the tibialis posterior and fibularis articles were behind a registration wall past that. Many results came as generated summaries with a few quoted lines, and the two Kenhub videos as transcript summaries. Each citation's qualification says which.
- **Search summaries can misquote.** Function claims rest on four to seven references from three to five publishers each. Lines a summary did not tie to a named page were not used (a 'knee-flexed' line that was the tool's own knowledge, Q7; an unattributed action row, Q37; an IMAIOS line, Q4). One StatPearls summary line about the plantaris read as a misquote and is not used (§6).
- **The key claim rests on abstracts.** The straight-knee versus bent-knee findings come from three EMG studies seen as abstracts or summaries (10 men in static efforts, at matched and at maximal effort; 48 adults in heel raises; 30 men in concentric efforts on a dynamometer). Their sizes differ and the claims say so; none of them measured growth.
- **Leverage is not force.** The cadaver moment-arm study (10 specimens) gives each muscle's leverage at the ankle and below it, not how hard the muscle works; it is cited for who can do what, and its numbers are said as leverage.
- **Automated review.** "Reviewed" here means checked by this automated process against search results. It is not a review by a clinician or an anatomist, and the database lane should not publish it as clinically verified on the strength of it.
- **Nothing here is hypertrophy evidence.** The selectivity claims and the force-sharing variation claim cite acute EMG studies. They say what was measured and that it is not growth, and they must never become set-credit weights. No growth study is cited.

Said plainly, as §8 requires on every region's "How this is counted":

> "Reviewed" means an automated agent checked each sentence against web-search excerpts and generated summaries of the cited sources on the date shown. It was not checked against the full texts, and not by a clinician or anatomist. A second automated review, with the same tools and limits, looked for errors; it is not peer review. The drawings are original schematics checked against these notes, not reviewed by an anatomist.

(The second automated review that sentence describes has been run on this dossier; §11 says what it checked and changed. It used the same tools and limits.)

### 3.1 The claims as worded

**`lower_leg.triceps_surae.function`** (function, established_anatomy, reviewed 2026-10-19)

> The calf muscles (triceps surae) are the gastrocnemius, with its two heads, and the soleus under it. Their tendons join in the Achilles tendon, which attaches to the heel bone, and together they point the foot down at the ankle (plantar flexion). In a cadaver study they were, with the long flexor of the big toe, the most important plantar flexors by leverage. One anatomy reference also credits them with a part in tilting the heel inward; in the same cadaver study the direction of their leverage below the ankle changed with the foot's position, so that action is not counted here.

- radiopaedia.triceps_surae, *Introduction* (Q9). Seen through web-search excerpts of the page, not the full text.
- statpearls.calf, *Introduction; Structure and Function* (Q4). Seen through a generated web-search summary of the page, not the page itself. The Bookshelf page (reported as NBK459362) was not opened; the statpearls.com copy was.
- statpearls.gastrocnemius, *Introduction; Structure and Function: the triceps surae* (Q5). Seen through web-search excerpts of the page, not the full text.
- elsevier.ankle_moment_arms, *Abstract* (Q49). Cadaver study of 10 specimens measuring leverage (moment arms), not muscle force or activity. Abstract only, seen through library records.

**`lower_leg.triceps_surae.variation.1`** (variation, experimental, reviewed 2026-10-19)

**`lower_leg.gastrocnemius.function`** (function, established_anatomy, reviewed 2026-10-19)

> The gastrocnemius points the foot down at the ankle (plantar flexion) and helps bend the knee. Its two heads start on the back of the thigh bone just above the knee and end through the Achilles tendon on the heel, so it crosses both the knee and the ankle. In a study that stimulated the muscle electrically in 17 people, its pull on the knee was greatest with the knee straight and small with the knee bent to 90 degrees.

**`lower_leg.gastrocnemius.selectivity.1`** (selectivity, experimental, reviewed 2026-10-19)

> Three studies measured how bending the knee changes the gastrocnemius's part in pointing the foot down. In 10 men, maximal plantar-flexion force fell as the knee bent, and at matched efforts gastrocnemius activity fell with it while soleus activity did not change; the authors estimated that the gastrocnemius gives at least two fifths of the force with the knee straight. In single-leg heel raises by 48 adults, gastrocnemius activity was slightly lower with the knee bent to 45 degrees than with it straight, a change the authors judged might be too small to affect clinical or muscle-specific outcomes. In 30 men pointing the foot against a machine with the knee bent to 90 degrees, force and inner-head activity were each about a third lower than with the knee straight. These are short-term activity measurements, not growth; they support describing the knee angle of a calf exercise, not a fixed split of credit.

- pmid.7498381, *Abstract: Methods and Results* (Q20). Short-term muscle activity in a controlled task; not a measurement of growth and not a set-credit weight. Static efforts by 10 men at matched and at maximal effort; abstract only.
- pmid.22190157, *Abstract: Methods, Results and Conclusions* (Q12, Q21). Short-term muscle activity in a controlled task; not a measurement of growth and not a set-credit weight. Single-leg heel raises by 48 healthy adults; abstract only.
- biologists.knee_position_plantarflexion, *Abstract: Methods and Results* (Q19, Q22). Short-term muscle activity in a controlled task; not a measurement of growth and not a set-credit weight. Concentric efforts on a dynamometer by 30 healthy men; inner head and soleus only. Seen through search summaries of the open-access article.

**`lower_leg.gastrocnemius.selectivity.2`** (selectivity, experimental, reviewed 2026-10-19)

**`lower_leg.soleus.function`** (function, established_anatomy, reviewed 2026-10-19)

> The soleus points the foot down at the ankle (plantar flexion). It starts on the shin bone and the fibula just below the knee and joins the gastrocnemius in the Achilles tendon, so it crosses the ankle but not the knee. One anatomy reference describes the two working together with the knee straight and, with the knee bent, the gastrocnemius's part as greatly reduced, so that the soleus acts as a primary plantar flexor. In muscle-activity studies, gastrocnemius activity was slightly lower in heel raises with the knee bent to 45 degrees, and inner-head activity about a third lower in machine efforts with the knee bent to 90 degrees. It also helps hold the body upright in standing. In an imaging study of 12 healthy people, its force-producing cross-section was about three times that of the inner gastrocnemius head and eight times that of the outer head.

**`lower_leg.soleus.selectivity.1`** (selectivity, experimental, reviewed 2026-10-19)

> The soleus starts just below the knee, so it does not cross the knee. Studies have compared its activity with the gastrocnemius's at different knee angles. In 10 men pushing the foot down at matched efforts, soleus activity stayed the same as the knee bent, while gastrocnemius activity fell. In single-leg heel raises by 48 adults, soleus activity was slightly higher with the knee bent to 45 degrees than with it straight. In 30 men with the knee bent to 90 degrees, soleus activity was higher in the first half of the slower movements. So in all three studies bending the knee lowered gastrocnemius activity, by amounts that differed between studies, while the soleus was active with the knee straight as well. These are short-term activity measurements, not growth, and not a fixed split of credit.

- **Status:** reviewed. Its function claim, lower_leg:lower_leg.tibialis_posterior.function, is reviewed.
- **Aliases:** tib post.
- **Location:** Back of the lower leg, deepest layer, against the back of the shin bone; its tendon passes behind the inner ankle bone and fans out under the foot.
- **What it does:** Tilts the sole of your foot inward and helps point the foot down. It helps hold up the inner arch of the foot, especially as you walk.
- **Shape note:** The deepest muscle at the back of the lower leg, under the calf muscles, so it has little or no contour of its own; the shape at the back of the leg comes from the muscles over it, body fat and individual anatomy.
- **Caveats:** Shown beside the calf muscles because of where it sits; lower-leg specialization does not target it. / Tilting the sole inward loads it together with the tibialis anterior.
- **Surface visibility:** `not_distinct`. The deepest muscle at the back of the leg, under the calf muscles (statpearls.tibialis_posterior, kenhub.tibialis_posterior).
- **Layer (§1.2):** deep. Supported: yes. The deepest muscle of the posterior compartment, lying directly against the posterior tibia (statpearls.tibialis_posterior; teachmeanatomy.posterior_compartment_leg; kenhub.tibialis_posterior); one of the deep group (statpearls.leg_posterior_compartment).
- **databaseMuscle (candidate):** `tibialis_posterior`. Exists in the canonical list (shared/capabilityRank.ts, mapped to no region).

## 6. Single-source claims, disagreements and what was not claimed

**Single-source claims.**

- None. Every function claim cites four or more references from at least three publishers: the calf (4 references, 3 publishers), gastrocnemius (4, 4: StatPearls, Kenhub, TeachMeAnatomy, one study), soleus (6, 5), tibialis anterior (6, 5), fibularis (7, 5), tibialis posterior (5, 5).
- Sentences inside function claims that rest on one reference: lower_leg.triceps_surae.function, 'the most important plantar flexors by leverage' and the changing direction of its leverage below the ankle (elsevier.ankle_moment_arms), and the inversion contribution (statpearls.gastrocnemius); lower_leg.gastrocnemius.function, the stimulation study (elsevier.gastrocnemius_knee_flexor); lower_leg.soleus.function, the knee-bent sentence (kenhub.soleus_video, a video transcript; worded as what is 'described', with the measured change on lower_leg.soleus.selectivity.1 and lower_leg.gastrocnemius.selectivity.1) and the cross-section sentence (pmid.1403308); lower_leg.tibialis_anterior.function, the measured leverage (elsevier.ankle_moment_arms); lower_leg.fibularis.function, 'weak' (radiopaedia.fibularis_longus) and 'eversion alone for the brevis' (teachmeanatomy.lateral_compartment_leg), each said as one reference's; lower_leg.tibialis_posterior.function, the arch-holding sentence (biomedcentral.tibialis_posterior_review; StatPearls also says the insertions help support the medial arch).
- Non-function claims resting on one source: lower_leg.triceps_surae.variation.1 (biologists.triceps_surae_force_sharing). Every selectivity claim cites three studies; every other variation claim two or three sources.
- Second review: lower_leg.soleus.function's knee-bent description still rests on one reference (kenhub.soleus_video) and is attributed to it; the measured sentence after it cites pmid.22190157 and biologists.knee_position_plantarflexion. lower_leg.tibialis_anterior.function's leverage sentence now cites two cadaver studies (elsevier.ankle_moment_arms, pmid.7951975). lower_leg.triceps_surae.variation.1 remains single-source (re-searched, SR11).

**Disagreements between sources, and how the wording handles them.**

- Straight-knee versus bent-knee calf work. The three studies agree in direction and differ in size: in static efforts at matched levels the gastrocnemius's activity fell with knee flexion and the soleus's did not change (pmid.7498381); in heel raises at 45 degrees the shift was small, about the same size in both directions, and the authors doubted it mattered (pmid.22190157); at 90 degrees in concentric efforts inner-head activity and force were about a third lower, and the soleus rose in the first half of slower movements (biologists.knee_position_plantarflexion). The claims say what each measured, on both nodes, and say that this supports describing knee angle, not a fixed split of credit. Kenhub's video states the textbook position (with the knee bent the soleus becomes the main plantar flexor); it is cited on the soleus's function claim, with the measured nuance on the selectivity claims.
- Studies not cited on the knee-angle claims: Signorile et al. 2002 (J Strength Cond Res 16(3):433-439; Q10) reported, per a truncated catalogue abstract, lower soleus activity with the knee straight than bent and no difference between the outer gastrocnemius head and the soleus at any angle; the excerpt was cut off and its methods not seen, so it is not cited. A KoreaScience study of 20 people (gastrocnemius activity changed from 30 degrees of knee flexion, the soleus did not; Q14, Q16) had no title, authors or year in the results, so it is not cited. Both point the same way as the cited studies. Arampatzis et al. 2006 (Q13) attributed the fall in gastrocnemius activity at strongly bent knees to the force-length state of all three muscles; not cited, since its journal was not shown.
- Turning the toes in or out (gastrocnemius heads): one study found a clear difference between heads (lww.heel_raise_foot_position), two smaller ones found no effect (mtprehab.heel_raise_leg_press, minerva.heel_raise_leg_rotation); the claim says the question is unsettled and the atlas counts one gastrocnemius.
- Tibialis anterior and inversion: StatPearls calls it primarily responsible for dorsiflexion and inversion and a primary inverter with the tibialis posterior; Kenhub calls it the main dorsiflexor that also inverts; a cadaver moment-arm study gave it about a fifth of the tibialis posterior's inversion leverage, everting in some specimens. The claim states the disagreement; the node action is assist on the measured evidence.
- Fibularis and plantar flexion: named for both muscles by Kenhub and StatPearls; 'weak' for the longus by Radiopaedia (a Radiopaedia case discussion, not cited, calls it a main action); TeachMeAnatomy lists eversion alone for the brevis; 'to a much lesser extent' than the triceps surae by moment arm. The claim says 'only a little' and names the disagreement; the node action is assist.
- Tibialis posterior and plantar flexion: StatPearls uses both 'primarily' and 'secondary'; Kenhub 'aids'; the moment-arm study 'to a much lesser extent'. The claim says it helps; the node action is assist.
- The triceps surae and inversion: StatPearls credits a contribution to subtalar inversion; the moment-arm study found the calf's subtalar leverage reverses with foot position. Said in the calf's function claim and kept out of the node actions.
- Gastrocnemius head length: the Kenhub video and a 32-leg cadaver study (medial longer in 28) agree that the inner head is usually the longer and reaches lower; the same study found the outer muscle-tendon junction higher in 25 of 32 legs, consistent with that. The appearance claim says 'usually'.
- Soleus size: Radiopaedia calls the soleus 'much larger' than the gastrocnemius, while Kenhub calls the gastrocnemius 'the main bulk of the calf'. Both hold (the soleus is the larger by volume and cross-section, pmid.1403308; the gastrocnemius is the visible bulk). The appearance claims say the gastrocnemius forms the main bulk at the surface and the soleus adds thickness under it.
- A StatPearls summary line said the plantaris is 'present in only a small share of people'. This looks like a misquote and is not used; the calf's caveat says only that some references count the plantaris in the group.
- Second review, soleus compensation at 90 degrees (SR3, SR4): Kovács et al. 2024 found no evidence that soleus activation made up for the medial gastrocnemius's lower activity; a later study of 17 men by some of the same authors (Ye et al., Physiology International 2025;112(3):369-379; seen through a repository listing and a summary, not cited) suggested the soleus may compensate at low speeds. The soleus selectivity claim now says neither.
- Second review, tibialis anterior and inversion (SR15, SR16): a second cadaver study (pmid.7951975, 15 specimens) gave the tibialis anterior about three fifths of the tibialis posterior's inverter leverage, against about a fifth in Klein et al.; the claim now gives both, and the node action stays assist as a closer call.
- Second review, foot position and the gastrocnemius heads (SR22, SR24, SR26): a further EMG study of 33 young adults (PubMed 28515978; title not shown, not cited) found toes turned out made the inner head more active and toes turned in made no difference between the heads; another, on IOS Press, matched the cited free-weight study's pattern. With the leg-press and seated studies finding no effect, 'unsettled' stands.
- Second review, the soleus's outcrops (SR5, SR7): Gray's sentence begins 'At the same time', and the condition it refers to was not found; the soleus appearance claim and node text say the edges 'can' show, and the outward projection rests first on Kenhub's German article.
- Second review, the soleus with the knee bent (SR14): independent results restating that the soleus becomes the main plantar flexor with the knee bent came only from exercise and coaching sites, one of which called the mechanism plausible but not firmly established; the soleus function claim attributes the statement to one anatomy reference and adds what two studies measured.

**Deliberately not claimed.**

- Tibialis posterior selectivity: a small MRI study (five healthy adults; pmid 15126722 in the results, Q43) found resisted foot adduction activated the tibialis posterior most selectively. It measured muscle-functional MRI signal, not EMG or a clinical finding, in five people, and the muscle is adjacent, not a target; not claimed.
- Fibre type of the soleus (mostly slow fibres): seen only in Wikipedia-derived summaries; not claimed.
- Calf hypertrophy studies by foot position or knee angle: growth evidence, outside what a selectivity claim may say; not searched for as evidence.

## 7. Deviations from §1.2

**D1. node actions: lower_leg.gastrocnemius at the knee (knee.flexion is not a lower-leg region action); the thigh's external listing of the gastrocnemius**

- *§1.2 fixes:* Lower-leg region actions: ankle.plantar_flexion, ankle.dorsiflexion, foot.inversion, foot.eversion. The thigh lists lower_leg.gastrocnemius as an external member, and 'a region lists another region's muscle when that muscle is a prime mover of one of the listing region's actions' (§1.2).
- *Evidence:* The gastrocnemius 'also assists with knee flexion' (statpearls.gastrocnemius, Q5); Kenhub and TeachMeAnatomy list knee flexion without a qualifier (kenhub.gastrocnemius, teachmeanatomy.posterior_compartment_leg); with the muscle stimulated electrically its knee-flexion moment was largest with the knee straight and small at 90 and 75 degrees (elsevier.gastrocnemius_knee_flexor). On the evidence the row is assist, not prime.
- *Assessment:* Two problems. (a) The externals rule names a prime mover; on the evidence the gastrocnemius is an assisting knee flexor, so the thigh's listing would rest on an assist row. (b) Node actions are global and declared by the home region, so the thigh can show the gastrocnemius's knee flexion only if the lower leg declares it; but compose.ts adds all of a region's own node actions to its page, and regionContent.test.ts refuses a page's node action whose action is not among the page's actions, so a knee.flexion row declared by the lower leg would fail the lower-leg page's test.
- *Proposal:* Not decided here; the row is recorded in nodeActionsOutsideRegion, not in nodeActions. Options for the review: (1) compose.ts filters a region's own node actions to its region actions as it already does for externals, and the lower leg declares the row; or (2) knee.flexion joins the lower leg's region actions (a §1.2 change, which would also put knee flexion on the lower-leg page); and, separately, the thigh lane either keeps the gastrocnemius as an external that assists knee flexion (the rule would need to say 'prime or assist'), or the externals rule stands and the thigh drops it. If a product reason wants the row prime, that is a product choice and should be said as one.

**D2. layer: lower_leg.fibularis ('surface')**

- *§1.2 fixes:* Fibularis longus and brevis as one node, layer 'surface'.
- *Evidence:* Fibularis longus lies superficially in the lateral compartment and is the larger, more superficial of the two; fibularis brevis lies deep to the longus (kenhub.fibularis_longus; teachmeanatomy.lateral_compartment_leg; statpearls.fibularis_brevis).
- *Assessment:* Not a contradiction: the node's belly at the surface is the longus's, and the drawing shows one node. But part of the node (the brevis) is under the surface; the shape note and visibility 'partial' say so.
- *Proposal:* No change to §1.2. The drawing lane draws one surface belly on the outer view and does not imply that both muscles are visible there; the diagram note says so.

## 9. Open questions

1. D1 (the gastrocnemius at the knee) needs a decision before the thigh's wave: where the knee.flexion row lives, and whether an assisting external is allowed.
2. No target muscle of the lower leg is prime for foot.inversion on the recorded evidence: the tibialis anterior is assist, and the only prime inverter is the tibialis posterior, an adjacent muscle whose rows do not drive suggestions. The page will therefore never name inversion as a missing main action. If it should, recording the tibialis anterior's inversion row as prime is a product choice, to be said as one (see that row's productNote). Second review: a second cadaver study gives the tibialis anterior about three fifths of the tibialis posterior's inward leverage (pmid.7951975), so assist is a closer call than first recorded; it stays assist, and the anatomy lane decides.

5. Muscles not in §1.2 that the references place in this region: the plantaris (superficial posterior; some references count it in the triceps surae), the popliteus, flexor digitorum longus and flexor hallucis longus (deep posterior), and the other three muscles of the front of the shin, which also lift the foot (teachmeanatomy.anterior_compartment_leg counts four; by standard anatomy they are the extensor digitorum longus, extensor hallucis longus and fibularis tertius, names not taken from the cited excerpts).
