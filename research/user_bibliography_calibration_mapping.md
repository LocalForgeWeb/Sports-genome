# User Bibliography: Calibration Mapping

## Review Method

The supplied bibliography was processed as a source-by-source verification queue. Each entry was classified as **verified** or **unresolved** and mapped to a calibration domain only when its design and population supported that use. The machine-readable study log is retained with the research workspace as `verify_user_study_bibliography.json` and records the verified citation, URL, population, relevant finding, mapping, and confidence for all 100 submitted entries.

## Implemented Calibration Rules

| App calibration area | Implemented rule | Evidence interpretation | Key sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypertrophy repetitions | Display **6–15** as a practical option, not a required zone | Reviews indicate hypertrophy can occur across a broad loading range when effort and technique are appropriate; this is a user-friendly planning anchor, not an optimum. | Currier et al. (2023); Schoenfeld et al. (2017) |
| Weekly hypertrophy volume | Retain **~10 weekly hard sets** as a starting reference and name **12–20** only as a study context for trained young men | Volume generally shows diminishing-return associations. The app does not infer an individual optimum or count all compound-lift exposure as full direct sets. | Schoenfeld et al. (2017); Baz-Valle et al. (2022); Pelland et al. (2026) |
| Strength repetitions and rest | Retain **1–6** as an advanced primary-work emphasis and describe **3 minutes** as a trained-athlete floor reference within a broader 3–5 minute planning range | Higher loads support strength specificity; longer rest can help preserve repeated high-force work. Neither establishes a one-size-fits-all prescription. | Currier et al. (2023); Grgic et al. (2018) |
| Frequency | Treat 1–3 weekly exposures as a way to distribute volume; do not claim frequency is independently superior when volume is equated | Frequency evidence is mixed for hypertrophy and volume-dependent for many strength findings. | Schoenfeld et al. (2016); Grgic et al. (2018, 2019) |
| Failure proximity | Keep RPE/RIR as coaching context rather than a required failure target | Meta-analytic findings do not support a universal failure mandate or exact RIR optimum. | Refalo et al. (2023); Robinson et al. (2024) |
| Range and muscle length | Preserve ROM and lengthened-loading as comparative exercise attributes, not direct hypertrophy percentages | Full ROM often compares favorably; long-length partial ROM can be useful in specific contexts. Direct exercise-specific longitudinal imaging studies take priority for regional claims. | Schoenfeld & Grgic (2020); Wolf et al. (2023); Maeo et al. studies |

## Evidence Boundaries

> **The app uses population-level planning anchors.** It does not measure one-repetition maximum, bar speed, force, electromyography, hypertrophy, readiness, injury risk, or individual recovery. Surface electromyography may inform an exercise-targeting estimate but cannot be converted into a percentage of force, hypertrophy, or muscle activation outcome.[1]

## Source Hierarchy Applied

For broad programming defaults, large recent meta-analyses, systematic reviews, and umbrella reviews receive priority. For exercise-specific muscle-region claims, a direct longitudinal imaging study is preferred over a broad general-training review because it is more direct for that question. Mechanistic, acute physiology, modeling, and EMG sources are used to explain uncertainty or compare attributes; they are not used to create false precision in user-facing scores.
